CAKIR — 438 Days Lost at Sea
Narration script (English)

[0:00] THE IMPOSSIBLE SURVIVOR

He went fishing in November 2012.
He would not see land again for 438 days.
No working engine. No way to call for help.
Just a small fishing boat, and the largest ocean on Earth.
When he finally reached a remote island on the other side of the Pacific, the story he told seemed almost impossible to believe.
His name is José Salvador Alvarenga.
This is what we know about his journey, what can be checked, and what rests on his word alone.

[0:38] AN ORDINARY FISHING TRIP

Alvarenga was born in El Salvador, in the coastal village of Garita Palmera.
In the early 2000s he moved to Mexico, and found work on the Pacific coast of Chiapas, fishing for sharks and other large fish from small open boats.
He had not been in touch with his family back home for years.
On November 17, 2012, he set out from the fishing village of Costa Azul, near Pijijiapan.
The plan was ordinary: a long shift at sea, perhaps thirty hours, and then home.
His usual partner could not come.
In his place came a younger man, Ezequiel Córdoba, whom Alvarenga barely knew.
Alvarenga was a seasoned fisherman. Córdoba, by most accounts, had very little experience at sea.
Their boat was a seven-meter fiberglass skiff, open to the sky, with a single outboard motor and a large icebox for the catch.

[1:39] THE STORM

Not long after they left, the weather turned.
According to Alvarenga, a storm pushed them far from the coast and battered the boat for days.
The engine failed. Much of their equipment was damaged or lost.
Without its engine, the open skiff was at the mercy of the waves.
To keep the boat stable, they threw their catch, hundreds of kilograms of fish, back into the sea.
Alvarenga later said he managed one radio call to his boss before the battery died.
Mexican rescue officials confirmed that a fishing boat was reported missing from Costa Azul two days after it sailed.
A search began. It was called off after two days, in poor visibility.
By then, the skiff was already being carried into the open Pacific.
No sail. No oars. No anchor. Nothing to steer with but the wind and the current.

[2:36] FIGHTING TO SURVIVE

Within days, the danger was no longer the storm. It was food, and water.
Alvarenga, the experienced fisherman, learned to catch fish with his bare hands as they swam beside the boat.
He caught seabirds that landed to rest. He caught sea turtles.
They pulled plastic bottles and other debris from the water, and turned them into tools and containers.
Sometimes, he later said, they found scraps of food floating past.
When rain came, the two men collected it in anything that would hold water.
When it did not, they drank what they could, including, he later said, turtle blood.
Every rainstorm mattered. Every catch was measured against the next empty day.
The icebox, built to keep fish cold, became their shelter from the sun and the waves.
On a boat with almost nothing, it was the closest thing they had to a roof.

[3:35] LOSING HIS COMPANION

The ordeal affected the two men differently.
According to Alvarenga, Córdoba struggled with the raw food from the beginning, and became ill.
As the weeks became months, he lost hope.
About four months into the drift, by Alvarenga's account, Córdoba stopped eating.
And then he died.
There were no witnesses. Everything we know about those weeks comes from the man who survived them.
Alvarenga has said he kept talking to his companion for days, before he finally let his body go into the sea.
He later said that he, too, thought about ending his life, and that his faith held him back.
From that day on, he was alone.

[4:20] ALONE IN THE PACIFIC

Alone, Alvarenga built routines.
He fished. He collected rain. He slept in the icebox.
He kept track of time not with a calendar, but with the moon, counting its cycles, one after another.
He said he saw large container ships pass in the distance. He waved and shouted. None of them stopped.
He dreamed of food, and of his family. He talked to himself, and he prayed.
Days of flat calm were followed by squalls. The sun burned by day.
Thousands of kilometers from any coast, he was far beyond the reach of any search.
At night, the sky was full of stars, and there was nothing between him and them.
Week after week, the current carried the skiff farther west, across the widest part of the Pacific.

[5:15] WAS THE JOURNEY POSSIBLE?

When the story became public, many people doubted it.
Could a man really survive more than a year in an open boat?
Skeptics asked how anyone could avoid scurvy for so long, without fruit or vegetables.
A physiologist at Duke University, Claude Piantadosi, explained that fresh meat from birds and turtles contains vitamin C, enough to prevent the disease if eaten in quantity.
Some details were hard to check, and a Mexican report from 2012 even listed the fishermen under different names.
His parents explained that in Mexico he was known by another name: Cirilo.
Scientists approached the question differently.
At the University of Hawaiʻi, oceanographers Nikolai Maximenko and Jan Hafner used a computer model of winds and ocean currents.
They released virtual drifters off the coast of Chiapas, and followed them for more than a year.
The drifters moved west in a remarkably narrow path, toward the Marshall Islands.
The researchers said Alvarenga's account fell well within the model's limits.
An oceanographer in Australia, Erik van Sebille, also said it was entirely possible for currents to carry a boat from Mexico to the Marshall Islands.
In April 2014, Alvarenga's lawyer said he had passed a polygraph test about the voyage.
None of this proves every detail of his story. But it shows that a journey like his was physically possible.

[6:52] LAND AT LAST

On January 30, 2014, after counting more than fifteen moons, Alvarenga saw land.
It was a tiny islet called Tile, at the edge of Ebon Atoll in the Marshall Islands, around ten thousand kilometers from where he had started.
He left the boat and swam to shore.
He came ashore near a small house belonging to a local couple.
Two local residents, Emi Libokmeto and Russel Laikidrik, found him exhausted and barely able to walk.
They took care of him, and the news reached the authorities.
Days later he was brought to the capital, Majuro, for medical care.
438 days after leaving Mexico, José Salvador Alvarenga was back among people.

[7:42] THE WORLD MEETS HIM

Within hours, his story was spreading around the world.
Doctors found him dehydrated, with low blood pressure and swollen ankles, but in better condition than many expected.
An American embassy official who questioned him said he found him truthful.
On February 10, 2014, he flew home to El Salvador, to a family that had not heard from him in years.
In Garita Palmera, his parents had long feared he was dead.
His daughter told reporters that the first thing she would do was hug him and kiss him.
In the months that followed, he struggled with sleep, and with a new fear of the sea.
In 2015, the journalist Jonathan Franklin told his story in detail in the book 438 Days.
Some questions about the voyage can only ever be answered by Alvarenga himself.

[8:37] THE COST OF SURVIVAL

José Salvador Alvarenga survived because he kept doing small things, again and again.
Catch the fish. Collect the rain. Count the moons.
But survival had a cost.
He lost his companion. He lost more than a year of his life.
And he came home a different man, from an ocean that had tried, for 438 days, to keep him.
Some parts of his story can be checked. Some never will be.
What remains is a man, a small boat, and an ocean that is still, for most of us, unimaginably large.
